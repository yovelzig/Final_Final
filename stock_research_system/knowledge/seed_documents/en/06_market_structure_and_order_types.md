# Market Structure and Order Types

> **Educational scope:** This document explains general financial concepts. It does not provide personalized investment advice, recommend a security, or guarantee a return. Order availability, trading sessions, settlement cycles, and investor protections differ by broker, venue, asset, and jurisdiction.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish primary and secondary markets.
- Explain the roles of exchanges, brokers, market makers, and electronic venues.
- Interpret bids, asks, spreads, liquidity, volume, and volatility.
- Compare market, limit, stop, and stop-limit orders as educational concepts.
- Recognize slippage, partial fills, non-execution, and trigger risk.
- Explain why displayed prices can differ from execution prices.
- Distinguish trade execution from settlement.
- Identify risks associated with regular and extended trading hours.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand stocks, ETFs, prices, and basic risk.

## Core Concepts

### Primary and secondary markets

The **primary market** distributes newly issued securities. In a typical issuance, the issuer receives capital, although an offering can also include sales by existing holders. The **secondary market** is where existing securities trade among investors after issuance. Secondary trading supports price discovery and liquidity but does not guarantee a buyer or seller at a desired price.

### Exchange, broker, and market maker

An **exchange** is an organized venue with published trading rules. A **broker** receives a customer instruction and arranges execution, often selecting among venues. A **market maker** is a dealer that stands ready to buy or sell at quoted prices for stated quantities. A security listed on one exchange can still be executed elsewhere when permitted.

### Bid, ask, spread, and depth

The **bid** is the highest displayed buying price for a stated quantity. The **ask** is the lowest displayed selling price for a stated quantity. The **spread** is ask minus bid. **Depth** describes quantities available at several price levels. A quote is a momentary snapshot, not a promise that unlimited quantity can trade there.

### Liquidity, volume, and volatility

**Liquidity** is the ability to trade promptly without causing a large price change. **Volume** is the quantity traded during a stated period. **Volatility** describes the size and frequency of price changes. These measures are related but not interchangeable: high daily volume can coexist with wide spreads or shallow depth at a particular moment.

### Market order

A market order instructs the broker to trade at the best prices then available. It emphasizes prompt execution rather than price control. The last trade and current screen quote are not guaranteed execution prices, especially when the order is large or the market is moving quickly.

### Limit order

A limit order sets a maximum purchase price or minimum sale price. A buy limit can execute only at the limit or lower; a sell limit can execute only at the limit or higher. Price protection creates non-execution and partial-fill risk. Reaching the limit price does not guarantee a fill because available size and order priority matter.

### Stop and stop-limit orders

A stop order is activated when a specified stop price is reached and commonly becomes a market order. A stop-limit order becomes a limit order after triggering. A stop price is a trigger, not a guaranteed execution price. A stop-limit adds price control but may remain unfilled.

### Slippage

**Slippage** is the difference between an expected reference price and the average execution price. It can reflect spread, changing quotes, limited depth, order size, latency, or fast markets. The reference must be stated clearly—for example, the quote at submission rather than a later price selected with hindsight.

### Trading hours

Markets can have regular sessions, auctions, and extended-hours sessions. Available venues, quotations, participants, order types, and liquidity may differ by session. Broker rules determine whether an order expires, remains queued, or is eligible for another session.

### Settlement

**Execution** is the agreement to trade at a price and quantity. **Settlement** is the later official transfer of securities and cash. Timing varies by asset and jurisdiction. As a dated example, most applicable U.S. broker-dealer securities transactions moved to T+1 on May 28, 2024, subject to exceptions.

## Detailed Explanation

### From instruction to execution

An investor normally sends an order to a broker rather than directly connecting to every market. The broker may route it to an exchange, market maker, electronic communications network, or another venue. Where permitted, the broker may also internalize the order. Venue rules, quotations, order size, routing, and market conditions influence the result.

Execution takes time, even when it appears instantaneous. Another order can trade first, displayed quantity can disappear, or only part of the requested quantity can fill. A useful review therefore records the instruction, quote and size at submission, session, individual fills, average price, and unfilled amount.

Brokers have execution duties under applicable rules, but best execution is not a promise that every order receives the best price visible after the event. Evaluation can include price opportunity, speed, and likelihood of execution; the legal standard is jurisdiction-specific.

### Priority and partial fills

Many venues rank orders by price and then time or another published rule. A market touching a limit price does not prove that every order at that price should fill. Earlier orders may have priority, and the quantity available may be smaller than the requested quantity.

A single order can execute in parts at different prices. The meaningful result is the volume-weighted average price plus any remaining quantity, not only the first fill.

### Market conditions and impact

Liquidity is a current condition, volume is completed activity, and volatility is price movement. During news or stress, volume and volatility can rise while usable liquidity becomes less reliable. A large order relative to available depth may consume several price levels and create **market impact**.

No execution method removes uncertainty. Dividing an order may reduce visible size but introduces delay and repeated exposure to price changes.

### Sessions, halts, and cancellations

Opening and closing auctions behave differently from continuous trading. Extended-hours sessions can have fewer participants, wider spreads, fragmented quotes, and order restrictions. Trading halts can interrupt execution.

A cancellation is another instruction that must be received and processed. The original order can execute before cancellation is confirmed, so actual status should be checked before a replacement is sent.

### Execution versus settlement

Trade date records the execution. Settlement date is when delivery of cash and securities is due. Account type, broker policy, asset class, and local rules affect what can be done before settlement. Because settlement cycles can change, the current rule should be verified rather than treated as universal.

## Integrated Concept Review

### A displayed price is a limited snapshot

A quote combines price, side, quantity, venue, and time. The last trade may no longer be available, and the best quote may cover only a small quantity. It can be a reasonable estimate for a small order in a liquid market but a weak estimate for a large order, volatile security, or thin session.

### Order types exchange one risk for another

A market order reduces ordinary non-execution risk but increases price uncertainty. A limit order controls price but can remain unfilled. A stop order automates activation but can execute far from its trigger. A stop-limit controls the post-trigger price but can fail to execute. The right educational question is which failure matters most: delay, non-execution, partial execution, or unfavorable price.

### Volume is not depth

Daily volume aggregates completed trades over time. Depth describes quantities currently offered at price levels. High reported volume therefore does not guarantee that a large order can execute near the current quote.

### Execution is not settlement

An execution confirmation answers what traded and at what price. Settlement completes the official exchange of securities and cash later. The applicable cycle depends on the asset, market, account, and date.

## Worked Examples

All amounts, securities, and outcomes below are hypothetical and are used only to demonstrate execution reasoning.

### Spread and immediate round-trip cost

A hypothetical stock shows a $24.90 bid and a $25.10 ask for 100 shares on each side. The displayed spread is:

\[
$25.10 - $24.90 = $0.20 	ext{ per share}
\]

A 100-share market buy that fills at $25.10 and an immediate market sell that fills at $24.90 would lose $20 before commissions, fees, taxes, or further price movement. This illustrates spread cost; it is not a forecast that both quotes will remain available.

### Market-order slippage and average price

The ask is $25.00 for 100 shares, $25.05 for 200 shares, and $25.15 for 200 shares. A hypothetical 500-share market buy fills across all three levels:

\]

The $25.08 average is above the first displayed ask because the order consumed multiple price levels.

### Limit price reached without a complete fill

A learner enters a buy limit for 500 shares at $19.50. Only 100 shares become available at $19.50 before the market rises. The order can receive a 100-share partial fill while 400 shares remain unfilled. Price protection worked, but full execution did not occur.

### Stop order during a gap

A sell stop at $40 is activated after overnight news, while the next available regular-session bids are near $35. Because the triggered instruction becomes a market order, the execution can be materially below $40. The stop price was a trigger, not a guaranteed sale price.

### Extended-hours limit order

A hypothetical regular-session quote was $50.00 bid and $50.05 ask. In an extended-hours session, the available quote is $49.50 bid and $50.60 ask with small size. A buy limit at $50.10 protects against paying $50.60, but it may remain unfilled. The example shows both wider spread and non-execution risk.

### Trade date and settlement date

A hypothetical market uses T+1 settlement for an applicable security. A trade executed on Monday is ordinarily due to settle on Tuesday, assuming both are business days and no exception applies. A holiday, different asset, or different jurisdiction can change the result, so the current rule must be verified.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming the last displayed trade is the price a market order will receive.
- Ignoring the quantity associated with the bid or ask.
- Treating daily volume as proof that a large order can execute without impact.
- Using a limit order without accepting partial fill or non-execution.
- Treating a stop price as a guaranteed execution price.
- Forgetting that order duration and session eligibility vary by broker.
- Sending a replacement before confirming that a cancellation succeeded.
- Equating zero commission with zero spread, impact, or other cost.
- Treating execution and settlement as the same event.

## Common Misconceptions

### The exchange listed on the security executes every order

A broker may route to another exchange, market maker, ECN, or internal venue, subject to applicable rules.

### High volume guarantees liquidity

Volume is completed activity over a period. Usable liquidity depends on current spread, depth, direction, and market conditions.

### A limit order is always safer than a market order

It protects the limit price but can leave the learner partially filled or entirely unfilled during a rapid move.

### A stop order guarantees a maximum loss

The stop is a trigger. Gaps and fast markets can produce a much worse execution price.

### A completed trade has already settled

Execution establishes the trade; settlement completes the official transfer later under the applicable cycle.

## Practical Application

Use this educational checklist before analyzing a hypothetical order:

1. State whether immediacy, price control, or full quantity is the priority.
2. Record side, quantity, order type, limit or stop values, and duration.
3. Identify the intended trading session and any session-specific restrictions.
4. Inspect bid, ask, spread, and displayed size rather than only last price.
5. Compare order size with current depth and recent volume without treating either as a guarantee.
6. Write an adverse scenario involving price movement, partial fill, non-execution, or a trading halt.
7. Confirm how the broker defines stop triggers and handles unfilled orders.
8. After execution, compare the benchmark quote with each fill and the average price.
9. Verify actual cancellation status before submitting a replacement.
10. Check the current settlement cycle and cash or delivery requirements for the relevant market.

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. **The issuer generally receives the capital, although an offering can also include sales by existing holders.**
2. **A primary market distributes newly issued securities; a secondary market trades securities that already exist.**
3. **An exchange provides an organized venue, a broker receives and routes customer instructions, and a market maker quotes prices as a dealer.**
4. **The learner does not know how much can be traded at that price or whether the quote can satisfy the intended order.**
5. **Volume measures completed trading over a period, while liquidity concerns the ability to trade promptly near current prices without substantial impact.**
6. **It does not guarantee the final execution price and may not execute normally during exceptional conditions.**
7. **The order can remain unfilled or only partially fill.**
8. **It commonly becomes a market order; a stop-limit instead becomes a limit order.**
9. **The requested quantity can exceed the quantity available at the best price, causing the order to consume additional price levels.**
10. **Execution is the agreement to trade; settlement is the later official exchange of securities and cash.**

## Key Takeaways

- Primary issuance and secondary trading serve different purposes.
- Brokers can route orders among exchanges, market makers, ECNs, or internal venues.
- Bid, ask, spread, and displayed size are momentary and limited information.
- Volume, liquidity, depth, and volatility are related but not interchangeable.
- Market orders emphasize immediacy; limit orders emphasize price control.
- Stop instructions create trigger risk and do not guarantee a maximum loss.
- Displayed prices can differ from fills because of size, routing, time, and changing conditions.
- Trading-session rules affect eligible orders, quotations, and liquidity.
- Execution and settlement are separate stages.
- No order type removes execution uncertainty.

## Glossary

- **Primary market:** Distribution of newly issued securities.
- **Secondary market:** Trading of existing securities.
- **Exchange:** Organized trading venue.
- **Broker:** Firm or person handling customer orders.
- **Market maker:** Dealer quoting buy and sell prices.
- **ECN:** Electronic system matching compatible orders.
- **Bid:** Displayed buying price for a stated quantity.
- **Ask:** Displayed selling price for a stated quantity.
- **Spread:** Ask minus bid.
- **Depth:** Quantity available at price levels.
- **Liquidity:** Ability to trade promptly with limited price impact.
- **Volume:** Quantity traded during a period.
- **Volatility:** Magnitude and frequency of price changes.
- **Partial fill:** Execution of only part of an order.
- **Slippage:** Difference between benchmark and average execution price.
- **Market impact:** Price effect associated with executing an order.
- **Trading halt:** Temporary suspension of trading.
- **Trade date:** Date of execution.
- **Settlement:** Official transfer of securities and cash.
