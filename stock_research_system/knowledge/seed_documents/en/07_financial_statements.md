# Financial Statements

> **Educational scope:** This document explains general financial-reporting concepts. It does not provide personalized investment advice, recommend a security, or guarantee a return. Reporting rules, filing forms, terminology, and presentation can differ by jurisdiction and accounting framework.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why companies prepare financial statements.
- Read the main sections of an income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow statement.
- Distinguish revenue, gross profit, operating income, and net income.
- Apply the accounting equation: assets equal liabilities plus shareholders' equity.
- Explain operating, investing, and financing cash flows.
- Trace how one transaction can affect more than one statement.
- Explain why accounting profit and cash flow are not identical.
- Identify limitations that require notes, management discussion, and comparison across periods.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand basic ideas about businesses, assets, liabilities, ownership, income, expenses, debt, and cash.

## Core Concepts

### Purpose of Financial Reporting

Financial reporting organizes information about a business's financial position, performance, and cash movements. The statements help investors, lenders, managers, employees, regulators, and other readers ask structured questions. They do not eliminate uncertainty or determine a company's market value by themselves.

The main statements answer different questions:

- The **income statement** reports performance over a period.
- The **balance sheet** reports financial position at a specific date.
- The **cash-flow statement** reports cash inflows and outflows over a period.
- A **statement of shareholders' equity**, when presented, explains changes in ownership accounts such as contributed capital, retained earnings, distributions, and certain other items.

These reports are connected. Reading only one can hide important facts visible in another.

## Detailed Explanation

### The Income Statement

### Revenue

Revenue is income recognized from a company's ordinary activities under the applicable accounting rules. Depending on the business, revenue may come from selling products, providing services, subscriptions, licenses, or other recurring activities.

Revenue is not automatically equal to cash received. A company can recognize a credit sale before the customer pays, creating an account receivable. Cash received in advance may also be recorded as a liability until the company performs the promised work.

**Question to ask:** What products, services, customers, regions, or contracts generated the revenue, and how much has actually been collected?

### Cost of revenue

Cost of revenue, sometimes called cost of sales or cost of goods sold, includes costs directly associated with producing or delivering the reported revenue. Examples can include materials, production labor, purchased merchandise, or certain service-delivery costs.

Classification varies across industries and accounting policies. A software company, retailer, manufacturer, and bank may present very different cost structures.

### Gross profit

Gross profit is calculated as:

**Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of revenue**

Gross profit shows the amount remaining after direct revenue-related costs, before operating expenses, financing costs, and taxes. It is not cash flow and it is not final profit.

### Operating expenses

Operating expenses support the business but are not classified as direct cost of revenue. They may include selling and marketing, research and development, administrative salaries, rent, depreciation, and other operating costs.

Some expenses involve current cash payments. Others, such as depreciation, allocate a previously recorded asset cost and do not require a new cash payment in the current period.

### Operating income

Operating income is generally calculated as:

**Operating income = Gross profit − Operating expenses**

It measures accounting profit from operations before items such as interest and income taxes, subject to the company's presentation and accounting framework.

### Net income

Net income is the final accounting profit or loss after recognized revenue, costs, other gains or losses, financing costs, and taxes. Positive net income does not guarantee that cash increased, that debt is manageable, or that the company is valuable at its current market price.

### The Balance Sheet

A balance sheet reports assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity at a specific date. Its organizing equation is:

**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity**

Every recorded transaction must preserve this equality, although individual accounts can change.

### Assets

Assets are resources recognized by the company. Common examples include:

- Cash and cash equivalents.
- Accounts receivable from customers.
- Inventory.
- Property, plant, and equipment.
- Investments.
- Intangible assets.

Assets may be classified as current or non-current. Current assets are generally expected to be realized, sold, or consumed during the operating cycle or within the relevant short-term classification period. Classification details depend on the applicable rules.

A reported asset value is an accounting amount, not necessarily the amount obtainable in an immediate sale. Estimates, impairment tests, depreciation methods, and historical cost can affect the carrying amount.

### Liabilities

Liabilities are recognized obligations. Examples include:

- Accounts payable to suppliers.
- Accrued expenses.
- Customer advances or deferred revenue.
- Loans and bonds payable.
- Lease obligations.
- Tax obligations.

Maturity matters. A company may report total assets greater than total liabilities while still facing a liquidity problem if cash is limited and obligations are due soon.

### Shareholders' equity

Shareholders' equity is the residual accounting interest after liabilities are subtracted from assets. It can include contributed capital, retained earnings, accumulated losses, treasury shares, and other components.

Net income generally increases retained earnings, while dividends or other distributions reduce it. Issuing shares can increase contributed capital. Repurchasing shares can reduce equity. Book equity is not the same as market capitalization.

### The Cash-Flow Statement

The cash-flow statement explains how cash and cash equivalents changed during a period. It classifies cash flows into operating, investing, and financing activities.

### Operating cash flow

Operating cash flow relates primarily to the company's revenue-producing operations. Under an indirect presentation, the operating section typically starts with net income and adjusts for:

- Non-cash expenses such as depreciation.
- Gains or losses classified elsewhere in the cash-flow statement.
- Changes in receivables, inventory, payables, and other operating accounts.

An increase in accounts receivable can reduce operating cash flow because recognized revenue has not yet been collected. An increase in accounts payable can temporarily increase operating cash flow because cash payment has been delayed.

Operating cash flow can be informative, but one period may be affected by timing, seasonality, unusual collections, delayed payments, or changes in working capital.

### Investing cash flow

Investing cash flow commonly includes purchases and sales of long-term assets and investments. Buying equipment is usually an investing cash outflow even though the equipment may support operations for years. Selling equipment may create an investing inflow.

Negative investing cash flow is not automatically bad. It may reflect productive expansion. Positive investing cash flow is not automatically good. It may reflect asset sales needed to raise cash.

### Financing cash flow

Financing cash flow reflects transactions with capital providers. Examples may include:

- Borrowing or repaying debt.
- Issuing or repurchasing shares.
- Paying dividends.

Borrowing creates cash but is not revenue. Debt repayment uses cash but is not an operating expense equal to the full principal payment. Interest treatment and classification can vary by accounting framework.

## Worked Examples

### One Internally Consistent Hypothetical Company

The following example uses a fictional company, **Harbor Light Bicycles, Inc.** All amounts are hypothetical and shown in U.S. dollars. The example is simplified for education and does not represent a real company.

### Opening balance sheet

At the beginning of the year, Harbor Light reports:

- Cash: $100,000.
- Accounts receivable: $60,000.
- Inventory: $80,000.
- Property and equipment, net: $250,000.

Total assets are $490,000.

It also reports:

- Accounts payable: $50,000.
- Debt: $200,000.
- Shareholders' equity: $240,000.

Liabilities plus equity equal $490,000, matching total assets.

### Income statement for the year

During the year, the company reports:

- Revenue: $1,000,000.
- Cost of revenue: $600,000.
- Gross profit: $400,000.
- Operating expenses: $260,000, including $30,000 of depreciation.
- Operating income: $140,000.
- Interest expense: $20,000.
- Income before tax: $120,000.
- Income tax expense: $30,000.
- Net income: $90,000.

The calculations are:

**Gross profit = $1,000,000 − $600,000 = $400,000**

**Operating income = $400,000 − $260,000 = $140,000**

**Net income = $140,000 − $20,000 − $30,000 = $90,000**

### Operating cash flow

Harbor Light starts with net income of $90,000 and makes these simplified adjustments:

- Add back depreciation of $30,000 because it reduced net income without a current-period cash payment.
- Subtract a $40,000 increase in accounts receivable because some recognized revenue was not collected.
- Subtract a $20,000 increase in inventory because cash was invested in additional inventory.
- Add a $15,000 increase in accounts payable because some supplier payments were deferred.

Therefore:

**Operating cash flow = $90,000 + $30,000 − $40,000 − $20,000 + $15,000 = $75,000**

### Investing and financing cash flow

The company purchases $100,000 of equipment, producing an investing cash outflow of $100,000.

It also:

- Borrows $60,000, a financing inflow.
- Pays $20,000 of dividends, a financing outflow.

Net financing cash flow is $40,000.

The total cash change is:

**Net cash change = $75,000 − $100,000 + $40,000 = $15,000**

Beginning cash was $100,000, so ending cash is $115,000.

### Closing balance sheet

At year-end, the simplified balance sheet reports:

- Cash: $115,000.
- Accounts receivable: $100,000.
- Inventory: $100,000.
- Property and equipment, net: $320,000.

The property and equipment balance reflects the $250,000 opening amount, plus $100,000 of purchases, minus $30,000 of depreciation.

Total assets are $635,000.

Liabilities are:

- Accounts payable: $65,000.
- Debt: $260,000.

Total liabilities are $325,000.

Shareholders' equity is $310,000. It increased from $240,000 by the $90,000 of net income and decreased by $20,000 of dividends:

**Ending equity = $240,000 + $90,000 − $20,000 = $310,000**

Liabilities plus equity equal $635,000, matching total assets.

## How the Three Statements Connect

The Harbor Light example shows several important links:

1. **Net income connects to equity.** The $90,000 profit increases retained earnings before distributions.
2. **Net income is the starting point for the simplified operating-cash reconciliation.** Non-cash items and working-capital changes explain why operating cash flow is $75,000 rather than $90,000.
3. **Capital expenditure connects cash flow and assets.** The $100,000 equipment purchase is an investing cash outflow and increases property and equipment.
4. **Depreciation connects the statements.** It reduces operating income and net income, is added back in the indirect operating-cash reconciliation, and reduces the carrying amount of equipment.
5. **Borrowing connects financing cash flow and liabilities.** The $60,000 loan increases cash and debt but does not create revenue.
6. **Dividends connect financing cash flow and equity.** They reduce cash and retained earnings but are not an operating expense in the income statement.
7. **Ending cash must agree.** The cash-flow statement's ending cash should reconcile to the balance sheet, subject to clearly explained presentation items.

## Notes, Management Discussion, and Audit Information

The face statements are summaries. Notes can explain accounting policies, estimates, debt terms, revenue recognition, commitments, contingencies, segments, taxes, leases, stock compensation, related parties, and other important matters.

Management's discussion and analysis may explain trends, known uncertainties, liquidity needs, and reasons for changes in results. It is management's perspective and should be compared with the statements and notes.

An auditor's report communicates the auditor's opinion and the framework and scope of the audit. An audit provides assurance under the applicable standards; it does not guarantee that a company will succeed, that every fraud will be detected, or that the securities are appropriately priced.

In the United States, a public company's Form 10-K generally includes audited annual financial statements, risk disclosures, and management discussion. EDGAR provides public access to SEC filings. Other jurisdictions use different forms and systems.

## Limitations of Financial Statements

Financial statements are essential but incomplete. Important limitations include:

- **Historical focus:** Statements mainly describe recognized past transactions and conditions.
- **Estimates and judgments:** Useful lives, impairments, provisions, credit losses, and fair values can depend on assumptions.
- **Accounting-policy differences:** Two companies can report economically similar activities differently within permitted rules.
- **Inflation and changing prices:** Historical carrying amounts may not reflect current replacement cost or market value.
- **Aggregation:** A total can hide differences among products, customers, regions, or subsidiaries.
- **Timing:** A strong or weak quarter may reflect seasonality or payment timing.
- **Non-financial factors:** Customer concentration, product quality, culture, regulation, and competitive position may not be fully captured.
- **Market value:** Book values and accounting earnings do not directly determine what investors should pay.

A reliable analysis therefore compares multiple periods, reads the notes, checks cash conversion, examines changes in accounting policies, and distinguishes reported facts from forecasts or interpretations.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating revenue as cash collected.
- Treating borrowing as revenue.
- Assuming net income equals operating cash flow.
- Reading a period-based income statement as though it were a point-in-time balance sheet.
- Ignoring working-capital changes.
- Treating all negative investing cash flow as harmful.
- Assuming positive shareholders' equity guarantees liquidity.
- Comparing companies without checking industry and accounting differences.
- Ignoring footnotes, audit language, and changes in estimates.

## Common Misconceptions

### A profitable company cannot run out of cash

A company can recognize profit while customers have not paid, inventory consumes cash, debt matures, or investments require funding.

### Depreciation is fake because no cash leaves this year

Depreciation is non-cash in the current period, but it allocates the recorded cost of a long-lived asset. The original asset purchase involved cash or financing, and future replacement may require cash.

### Higher revenue always means a stronger business

Revenue growth can coincide with falling margins, poor collection, customer concentration, or heavy spending. The quality and cost of growth matter.

### The balance sheet shows what the company could be sold for

The balance sheet uses accounting measurements. Market value can differ substantially because of future expectations, unrecognized assets, risk, and market conditions.

## Practical Application

Choose one public company's annual filing from an official filing system. Do not begin with a price target. Instead:

1. Identify the reporting period and accounting framework.
2. Locate revenue, cost of revenue, gross profit, operating income, and net income.
3. Locate cash, receivables, inventory, debt, and shareholders' equity.
4. Reconcile the beginning and ending cash amounts using the cash-flow statement.
5. Identify the largest operating-cash adjustment.
6. Identify the largest investing and financing cash flows.
7. Read the notes related to revenue, debt, and significant estimates.
8. Write three factual observations and three unanswered questions.

This exercise trains source reading. It does not by itself establish whether the security is suitable or fairly valued.

## Key Takeaways

- Each primary statement answers a different question.
- Revenue, profit, and cash are related but not interchangeable.
- Assets must equal liabilities plus shareholders' equity.
- Operating, investing, and financing cash flows should be interpreted in context.
- Transactions often affect multiple statements.
- Notes and management discussion are necessary for understanding the summaries.
- Financial statements are evidence for analysis, not a complete valuation or forecast.

## Glossary

- **Accrual accounting:** Recognition based on economic activity under accounting rules rather than only cash movement.
- **Asset:** A recognized economic resource.
- **Liability:** A recognized obligation.
- **Shareholders' equity:** Residual accounting interest after liabilities are subtracted from assets.
- **Revenue:** Income recognized from ordinary activities.
- **Cost of revenue:** Direct costs associated with producing or delivering reported revenue.
- **Gross profit:** Revenue minus cost of revenue.
- **Operating expense:** A recognized cost of running the business outside direct cost of revenue.
- **Operating income:** Gross profit minus operating expenses, subject to presentation rules.
- **Net income:** Final accounting profit or loss after recognized items.
- **Accounts receivable:** Amounts customers owe the company.
- **Accounts payable:** Amounts the company owes suppliers.
- **Depreciation:** Allocation of the recorded cost of a long-lived tangible asset over periods.
- **Operating cash flow:** Cash flow primarily associated with revenue-producing operations.
- **Investing cash flow:** Cash flow associated with long-term assets and investments.
- **Financing cash flow:** Cash flow associated with debt and equity financing sources.
- **Working capital:** Current operating assets and liabilities considered together.
