# Risk, Volatility, and Drawdown

> **Educational scope:** This document explains general financial concepts. It does not provide personalized investment advice, recommend a security, or guarantee a return.

## Learning Objectives

After studying this document, a learner should be able to:

- Define investment risk in relation to a financial objective rather than as one number.
- Explain volatility and standard deviation at a conceptual level.
- Distinguish downside risk, drawdown, maximum drawdown, and permanent capital loss.
- Calculate a peak-to-trough drawdown and the gain required for recovery.
- Identify liquidity, credit, interest-rate, inflation, currency, concentration, sequence-of-returns, and leverage risks.
- Distinguish risk capacity from willingness to take risk.
- Explain why historical risk measurements are informative but cannot set a guaranteed limit on future losses.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand percentages, investment returns, stocks, bonds, cash, and basic portfolio diversification. Familiarity with arithmetic averages is helpful but not required.

## Core Concepts

### Risk Is Goal-Related Uncertainty

Investment risk is uncertainty that can harm financial welfare or prevent a goal from being achieved. A useful risk statement identifies the objective, the adverse event, and the consequence. “Prices may fluctuate” is incomplete; “a market decline before a required tuition payment could force a sale at a loss” connects market behavior to a real failure condition.

Risk is broader than volatility. An investment can show small reported price changes while containing credit, liquidity, leverage, inflation, currency, or concentration risk. No single metric captures every relevant form of risk.

### Volatility and Standard Deviation

Volatility describes how widely prices or returns move over time. Higher volatility can increase the chance that an asset is below its starting value when money is needed, but volatility treats gains and losses as variation. A large gain can raise measured volatility even though it is not itself a loss.

Volatility estimates depend on observation frequency, the historical period, whether distributions are included, and the measurement currency. Figures should be compared only when these choices are consistent.

Standard deviation summarizes how dispersed observations were around their average. Conceptually, calculate the average return, find each return’s distance from the average, square those distances, average them, and take the square root. The exact formula differs for a population and a sample. The essential point is that standard deviation summarizes historical dispersion; it does not state the maximum possible loss. It can also understate risk when prices are stale, liquidity is limited, leverage is present, or extreme events are poorly represented in the sample.

### Downside Risk

Downside risk focuses on outcomes below a defined threshold, such as zero return, an inflation-adjusted target, or a required spending level. The threshold must be explicit because the same history can produce different conclusions under different objectives.

### Drawdown and Maximum Drawdown

A drawdown is the percentage decline from a previous peak:

\[
\text{Drawdown} =
\frac{\text{Current Value} - \text{Prior Peak Value}}
{\text{Prior Peak Value}}
\]

The result is negative during a decline, although some reports show the positive loss magnitude. Maximum drawdown is the deepest observed peak-to-trough decline during a selected period. It is path-dependent: a portfolio may finish above its starting value while experiencing a severe decline along the way.

Historical maximum drawdown is not a guaranteed worst-case limit. A future decline may be deeper, last longer, or occur with worse liquidity than the selected history.

### Permanent Capital Loss

A drawdown can be temporary if value later recovers, but recovery is never assured. Permanent capital loss occurs when capital is not restored within the relevant objective or horizon. Causes can include default, business failure, fraud, dilution, forced liquidation, or paying a price that future cash flows do not justify.

The distinction is often uncertain in real time. Evaluating the underlying cause, financial condition, time horizon, and liquidity need is more useful than automatically labeling every decline temporary or permanent.

## Detailed Explanation

### Liquidity Risk

Liquidity risk is the possibility that an asset cannot be sold quickly, in the desired quantity, at a reasonable price. Normal trading volume does not guarantee liquidity during stress. Portfolio liquidity also matters: even liquid assets can become a problem when cash needs exceed reserves and a sale must occur at an unfavorable time.

Useful questions include how soon cash may be needed, how much can be sold without materially moving the price, whether lockups or settlement delays apply, and what may happen if many holders sell together.

### Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that a borrower or counterparty will not pay in full or on time. A bond can lose value before default if perceived creditworthiness deteriorates. Higher promised yield may compensate for greater default risk, lower liquidity, or other uncertainty rather than represent a free benefit.

### Interest-Rate Risk

Existing fixed-rate bond prices generally move inversely to market yields. When new bonds offer higher yields, an older lower-coupon bond usually needs a lower price to remain competitive. Longer maturities and lower coupons generally create greater price sensitivity. Holding to maturity reduces concern about interim price movement only when the issuer pays as promised and the investor does not need to sell.

### Inflation Risk

Inflation risk is the possibility that nominal money loses purchasing power. A 3% nominal gain alongside 5% inflation produces more dollars but less purchasing power. Risk analysis should therefore distinguish nominal returns from real, inflation-adjusted outcomes.

### Currency Risk

Currency risk arises when an asset, liability, income stream, or spending goal uses a different currency from the investor’s measurement currency. A foreign investment can gain locally yet lose after conversion. Currency exposure may also be indirect through foreign revenue, costs, or debt. Hedging can reduce selected exposure but adds cost and may not eliminate every effect.

### Concentration Risk

Concentration risk is amplified loss caused by excessive dependence on one security, issuer, industry, country, factor, employer, or economic outcome. It may be hidden across accounts or overlapping funds. Ten holdings are not necessarily diversified when they respond to the same driver, and concentration can grow unintentionally when one position appreciates faster than the rest.

### Sequence-of-Returns Risk

Sequence-of-returns risk is the effect of return order when money is contributed or withdrawn. Without cash flows, the same returns in reverse order lead to the same ending value. With withdrawals, an early loss can be more damaging because the withdrawal removes a larger share of the remaining capital. Average return alone is therefore insufficient when cash flows occur along the path.

### Leverage Risk

Leverage uses borrowed money or contractual exposure to control a position larger than the investor’s equity. It magnifies gains and losses, adds financing costs, and can create margin or collateral calls. Market gaps and declining liquidity may force sales at unfavorable prices. The SEC warns that margin investors can lose more than the amount deposited and that securities may be sold to satisfy margin requirements.

### Risk Capacity Versus Willingness

Risk capacity is the financial ability to withstand loss or delay without failing an objective. It depends on time horizon, income stability, liquidity needs, debt, obligations, reserves, and reliance on the portfolio.

Risk willingness is the psychological readiness to accept uncertainty and declines. The two can conflict: confidence does not create financial capacity, and high capacity does not guarantee that a person will remain committed during stress. A workable risk level must respect the more restrictive constraint.

## Worked Examples

All values and outcomes below are hypothetical and are used only for education.

### Example 1: Drawdown Calculation

A portfolio reaches a peak of $120,000 and later falls to $90,000.

\[
\frac{\$90{,}000 - \$120{,}000}{\$120{,}000} = -0.25 = -25\%
\]

The portfolio is in a 25% drawdown from that peak. The dollar decline is $30,000.

### Example 2: Maximum Drawdown Across a Path

Consider the following year-end values:

- Year 0: $100,000
- Year 1: $115,000
- Year 2: $92,000
- Year 3: $105,000
- Year 4: $121,000

The decline from the Year 1 peak of $115,000 to the Year 2 trough of $92,000 is:

\[
\frac{\$92{,}000 - \$115{,}000}{\$115{,}000} = -20\%
\]

The portfolio later reaches a new peak. The maximum drawdown in this short path is 20%, even though the ending value is higher than the starting value.

### Example 3: Recovery Is Asymmetric

A 40% loss turns $100 into $60. Returning from $60 to $100 requires a gain of $40 on a $60 base:

\[
\frac{\$100 - \$60}{\$60} = 66.67\%
\]

The required recovery percentage is larger than the loss percentage because the gain begins from a smaller base.

### Example 4: Sequence Risk With Withdrawals

Two hypothetical portfolios begin with $100,000 and experience +20% and −20%, but in different orders. Each portfolio pays a $10,000 withdrawal after the first year.

**Portfolio A: +20% first**

- After return: $120,000
- After withdrawal: $110,000
- After −20%: $88,000

**Portfolio B: −20% first**

- After return: $80,000
- After withdrawal: $70,000
- After +20%: $84,000

The same two annual returns lead to different ending balances because the withdrawal occurs between them.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating volatility as a complete definition of risk.
- Comparing standard deviations calculated from different frequencies or periods.
- Assuming a low-volatility price history proves an asset is safe.
- Using historical maximum drawdown as a guaranteed future limit.
- Ignoring inflation because the nominal account balance increased.
- Evaluating each account separately and missing total-portfolio concentration.
- Assuming an asset will be liquid during a market-wide rush to sell.
- Ignoring withdrawals when comparing return sequences.
- Treating leverage as only a return enhancer and overlooking forced-sale risk.
- Using willingness to take risk as a substitute for financial capacity.

## Common Misconceptions

### “A diversified portfolio cannot experience a major drawdown”

Diversification can reduce security-specific or segment-specific risk, but broad shocks can affect many holdings together. Correlations may also rise during stress.

### “A decline is only a paper loss until it is sold”

An unrealized decline can still impair borrowing capacity, trigger collateral requirements, reduce future spending options, or become permanent. The need to sell is one factor, not the only factor.

### “Low volatility means low risk”

Infrequent pricing, illiquidity, credit exposure, leverage, or rare severe losses can produce deceptively smooth reported returns.

### “A 20% loss needs a 20% gain to recover”

After a 20% loss, $100 becomes $80. A $20 gain on $80 is 25%, so a 25% return is needed to recover.

### “Risk tolerance is just personality”

Willingness matters, but the ability to absorb loss depends on financial obligations, reserves, time horizon, and reliance on the assets.

## Practical Application

A learner can use the following educational risk review without treating it as personalized advice:

1. State the goal, amount, date, and currency.
2. Define the events that would cause the goal to fail.
3. Separate capacity to bear loss from willingness to experience volatility.
4. Calculate current drawdown from the latest peak and identify the historical maximum drawdown period.
5. List market, liquidity, credit, interest-rate, inflation, currency, concentration, sequence, and leverage exposures.
6. Examine exposures across all accounts and underlying holdings.
7. Test hypothetical shocks rather than relying only on average returns.
8. Include cash needs, withdrawals, financing costs, and possible forced sales.
9. Record assumptions, data period, return frequency, and measurement currency.
10. Revisit the analysis when objectives, obligations, holdings, or market structure change.

Stress testing is not a prediction. A stress scenario is a conditional question such as: “What happens if equity prices fall 30%, credit spreads widen, the local currency weakens, and a planned withdrawal still occurs?” Its value comes from exposing dependencies and failure points before a real shock.

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. **Risk includes any uncertainty that can impair an objective, including credit, liquidity, inflation, currency, concentration, sequence, and leverage risks.** Volatility measures variability and therefore covers only part of the risk picture.
2. **It summarizes how dispersed historical observations were around their average.** It does not state the maximum possible future loss.
3. **The drawdown is 25%.** The calculation is ($120,000 − $160,000) / $160,000 = −25%.
4. **It describes only the deepest observed decline in a selected historical period.** Future conditions, leverage, liquidity, and market relationships can differ.
5. **Liquidity risk concerns the ability to sell or obtain cash at a reasonable price and time; credit risk concerns failure or deterioration in the ability of a borrower or counterparty to pay.** The risks can interact.
6. **New bonds may offer higher yields, so an existing lower-coupon bond generally needs a lower price to remain competitive.** Sensitivity depends on maturity, coupon, and other features.
7. **It is the effect of return order when contributions or withdrawals occur.** Early losses can be especially damaging when withdrawals reduce the remaining capital.
8. **Leverage creates exposure larger than the investor’s equity, so a given underlying price move produces a larger percentage gain or loss on that equity.** It may also trigger financing costs and forced sales.
9. **Capacity is the financial ability to absorb loss without failing an objective; willingness is the psychological readiness to tolerate uncertainty and declines.** A suitable educational risk assessment considers both.

## Key Takeaways

- Define risk in relation to a financial objective and a failure condition.
- Volatility and standard deviation describe historical variation, not every form of risk.
- Drawdown is measured from a prior peak; maximum drawdown is the deepest observed peak-to-trough decline in a period.
- Recovery percentages are larger than equal-sized loss percentages.
- Permanent loss, liquidity, credit, rates, inflation, currency, concentration, sequence, and leverage require separate analysis.
- Cash flows make the order of returns important.
- Risk capacity and willingness are different and can conflict.
- Historical measurements and stress scenarios organize uncertainty but cannot guarantee future outcomes.

## Glossary

- **Risk:** Uncertainty that can negatively affect financial welfare or prevent an objective from being achieved.
- **Volatility:** Degree of variation in prices or returns over time.
- **Standard deviation:** Statistical measure of dispersion around an average.
- **Downside risk:** Risk of outcomes below a defined threshold.
- **Drawdown:** Percentage decline from a prior peak to a later value before a new peak.
- **Maximum drawdown:** Deepest observed peak-to-trough decline during a selected period.
- **Permanent capital loss:** Loss that is not recovered within the relevant objective or horizon.
- **Liquidity risk:** Risk that cash cannot be obtained through sale or funding at an acceptable time and cost.
- **Credit risk:** Risk that a borrower or counterparty fails to meet obligations or becomes less creditworthy.
- **Interest-rate risk:** Risk that changing market rates affect investment value or income.
- **Inflation risk:** Risk that money or returns lose purchasing power.
- **Currency risk:** Risk arising from changes in exchange rates between relevant currencies.
- **Concentration risk:** Risk of amplified loss from excessive dependence on one exposure or related group of exposures.
- **Sequence-of-returns risk:** Risk that the order of returns interacts with contributions or withdrawals.
- **Leverage:** Use of borrowing or contractual exposure to control assets larger than the investor’s equity.
- **Risk capacity:** Financial ability to withstand adverse outcomes.
- **Risk willingness:** Psychological willingness to accept uncertainty and losses.
- **Stress test:** Conditional analysis of specified adverse events.
