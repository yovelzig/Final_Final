# Investment Thesis and Decision Journal

> **Educational scope:** This document teaches a general research and decision-review process. It does not recommend any security, provide personalized investment advice, or guarantee a return. Every company, price, probability, and outcome in the examples is hypothetical.

## Learning Objectives

After studying this document, a learner should be able to:

- Write a concise and falsifiable investment thesis.
- Separate verified evidence, interpretation, forecasts, and assumptions.
- Describe the expectation that may already be reflected in a market price.
- Build base, favorable, and adverse scenarios without pretending that probabilities are certain.
- Define milestones, review triggers, and thesis-breakers before an outcome is known.
- Create a timestamped decision-journal entry that preserves the original information set.
- Evaluate process quality separately from profit or loss.
- Use post-decision reviews to improve calibration without rewriting history.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand financial statements, fundamental analysis, valuation ranges, risk and return, diversification, drawdown, and common behavioral biases. The learner should also know that a market price is an observable transaction price, while intrinsic value is an uncertain estimate based on assumptions.

## Core Concepts

### Investment thesis

An **investment thesis** is a structured, testable explanation of why an asset's future economics may differ from expectations reflected in its price. It connects business drivers, evidence, valuation, risks, and a review plan. It is not merely a favorable story or an exact-price prediction.

A useful thesis answers: What appears to be expected? What does the analysis expect to differ? What evidence would support or weaken that view? What risks could make the decision unacceptable?

### Market expectation and variant perception

A **market expectation** is the set of assumptions that appears consistent with the current price, available forecasts, or prevailing narrative. It cannot usually be observed perfectly, so the analyst explains how it was inferred.

A **variant perception** is a supported interpretation that differs from that expectation. Disagreement alone is not an advantage. The difference must be material, evidence-based, and testable. It may concern direction, magnitude, probability, or timing.

### Evidence, interpretation, and assumption

**Evidence** is verifiable information relevant to a claim, such as audited statements, regulatory filings, contracts, or dated operating metrics. **Interpretation** explains what evidence may mean. An **assumption** is an uncertain input accepted for analysis, such as future growth, margin, refinancing cost, or retention. These categories should remain separate because they have different levels of certainty.

### Scenario and probability-weighted value

A **scenario** is a coherent set of assumptions describing one possible path. A base scenario is a central working case, not a promise. Favorable and adverse scenarios expose sensitivity and asymmetry.

A simple probability-weighted value is:

\[
EV = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i V_i
\]

where \(EV\) is estimated dollars per share, \(p_i\) is the decimal probability of scenario \(i\), and \(V_i\) is that scenario's dollars-per-share value. Probabilities sum to 1.00. The calculation organizes subjective assumptions; it does not make them objective.

### Catalyst, milestone, review trigger, and thesis-breaker

A **catalyst** may cause participants to revise expectations. A **milestone** shows whether the operating thesis is progressing. A **review trigger** requires reassessment. A **thesis-breaker** invalidates a central assumption, removes the expected source of value, or violates an essential risk rule.

Price movement alone usually calls for checking facts and risk; it does not prove that the thesis is correct or broken.

### Decision journal

A **decision journal** is a timestamped record of information, alternatives, forecasts, uncertainties, and rules before a decision. Later observations are appended with new dates rather than inserted into the original entry.

Research on hindsight bias shows that outcome knowledge can alter perceived predictability. Research on outcome bias shows that favorable results can improve ratings of decision quality even when the prior information was otherwise identical. Preserving the foresight record helps reduce these distortions during review.

## Detailed Explanation

### 1. Define the decision and information cutoff

Record the instrument, objective, horizon, decision date, and exact information cutoff. Also record alternatives such as no action, waiting, using a diversified vehicle, or using less hypothetical exposure. A journal containing only the chosen idea hides opportunity cost.

### 2. Build an evidence ledger

For each important claim, record the source, filing or document type, publication date, period covered, direct link, and whether it is audited, unaudited, management guidance, third-party analysis, or the learner's estimate.

For a U.S. public company, EDGAR may provide Forms 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K. The SEC emphasizes reading financial statements and footnotes together because no single statement tells the complete story. Ten articles repeating one press release are not ten independent sources; primary documents should anchor factual claims.

### 3. Write a causal chain

Connect the thesis as:

**driver -> operating result -> financial result -> valuation implication**

For example, shorter deployment time may improve adoption; adoption may increase recurring revenue; scale may improve margin; and stronger cash generation may support a higher valuation range. Every arrow contains an assumption that should be monitored.

Replace vague labels such as "great company" or "large market" with observable claims. State what behavior demonstrates quality and what evidence would show the advantage weakening.

### 4. State expectation and variant view separately

A variant view needs a comparison point. The expectation may be inferred from valuation, guidance, published estimates, or the operating results required to justify a hypothetical price. Each method is imperfect.

Write two separate sentences: one for the apparent expectation and one for the variant view. Then explain the evidence and uncertainty behind both. Do not treat "the market" as one person with one forecast.

### 5. Expose assumptions and sensitivity

For each high-impact assumption, record its value, unit, period, source, plausible range, milestone, and consequence if wrong. Sensitivity analysis shows how the conclusion changes when assumptions change; it does not assign probabilities. A conclusion that disappears after a small plausible change is fragile.

### 6. Separate scenarios from risk capacity

A scenario describes possible economics. Risk capacity describes how much loss, illiquidity, concentration, or uncertainty a hypothetical portfolio can withstand. Favorable expected value does not justify unlimited exposure.

Use rounded probability estimates, explain their basis, ensure they total 100%, and include adverse financing, dilution, competition, execution, and liquidity conditions. Avoid false precision.

### 7. Predefine monitoring and invalidation

Separate routine monitoring, review triggers, and thesis-breakers. This avoids both never changing the thesis and changing it after every price move. Criteria should be specific, while allowing investigation of data quality and context.

### 8. Preserve the pre-decision snapshot

Before commitment, record the one-paragraph thesis, unresolved questions, scenarios, valuation date, exposure rule, liquidity assumptions, milestones, thesis-breakers, confidence, possible biases, alternatives, and next review date. Lock the original entry; append corrections with timestamps.

### 9. Review process separately from outcome

At review, compare expected drivers with observed facts and ask what was knowable at the time. Classify differences as information error, model error, assumption error, execution error, behavioral error, or normal uncertainty.

A profit may follow weak research and unrelated news. A loss may follow a sound process and a low-probability event. The classification is educational and cannot perfectly explain every outcome.

### 10. Improve calibration over repeated decisions

Calibration compares stated confidence with observed frequency across many comparable decisions. One result cannot validate a 70% forecast. Over a sufficiently large, consistently defined sample, events assigned about 70% confidence should occur about 70% of the time. The goal is to make uncertainty visible, not eliminate it.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Falsifiable thesis

**Hypothetical entity:** Northstar Workflow Software. This is not a real-company recommendation.

Weak: "Northstar is innovative and should grow."

Stronger: "As of July 26, 2026, reduced implementation time is expected to improve retention and operating margin over the next six reported quarters. The thesis is supported if implementation time, retention, and operating cash flow improve together. It weakens if implementation improves but retention does not, and it breaks if customer losses rise for two consecutive periods while management withdraws its efficiency plan."

The stronger version identifies a date, mechanism, metrics, horizon, and invalidation condition.

### Example 2: Probability-weighted value

Assume hypothetical one-year values:

- adverse: 30% probability and $40 per share;
- base: 50% probability and $70 per share;
- favorable: 20% probability and $100 per share.

\[
EV = (0.30 \times \$40) + (0.50 \times \$70) + (0.20 \times \$100) = \$67
\]

The $67 result is an assumption-weighted estimate, not a promise. The journal records why each probability and value was selected.

### Example 3: Outcome versus process

A learner ignores primary sources and an exposure limit, then profits from unrelated acquisition news. The outcome is favorable, but the process contains information, behavioral, and execution errors.

Another learner follows a documented process but loses after an unexpected regulatory event. Review may identify normal uncertainty while still asking whether the event was discoverable or underweighted.

## Common Mistakes

- Writing a promotional narrative instead of a falsifiable claim.
- Listing only evidence that supports the preferred conclusion.
- Treating management guidance as verified fact rather than a forward-looking statement.
- Failing to record the information cutoff and source dates.
- Using the current price as the only measure of thesis progress.
- Calling every disappointing metric a thesis-breaker, or defining no thesis-breaker at all.
- Assigning precise probabilities without a defensible basis.
- Ignoring financing, dilution, liquidity, concentration, fees, and taxes where relevant.
- Editing the original journal entry after learning the outcome.
- Reviewing one successful decision and concluding that confidence estimates are calibrated.

## Common Misconceptions

### "A detailed thesis is accurate"

Detail exposes assumptions but does not make them correct. Testability matters more than length.

### "Variant perception means being contrarian"

An unpopular view is not valuable unless it is supported, material, and relevant to price expectations.

### "A catalyst creates value"

A catalyst may reveal information or change attention; it does not by itself improve cash flows or reduce risk.

### "A thesis-breaker is always a stop-loss price"

Price may be a risk input, but analytical invalidation usually concerns economics, financing, governance, evidence, or a portfolio rule.

### "A profitable decision proves skill"

Outcome bias can validate weak reasoning after a favorable result. Skill requires repeated evaluation of process, risk, and calibration.

### "The journal should eliminate emotion"

Emotion cannot always be removed. Recording urgency, fear, excitement, or attachment makes possible influence easier to detect.

## Practical Application

Use this educational template before a hypothetical decision:

1. **Decision and timestamp:** What is considered, and what is the information cutoff?
2. **Objective, horizon, and alternatives:** What is the purpose, review period, and opportunity cost?
3. **Expectation and variant view:** What appears priced in, and what differs?
4. **Evidence ledger:** Which dated primary and secondary sources support each claim?
5. **Causal chain:** How do drivers connect to financial results and valuation?
6. **Assumptions and sensitivity:** Which inputs matter most, and what changes the conclusion?
7. **Scenarios:** What are the adverse, base, and favorable cases with rounded probabilities?
8. **Risk rules:** What hypothetical exposure, concentration, liquidity, and loss limits apply?
9. **Milestones and thesis-breakers:** What causes monitoring, review, or invalidation?
10. **Bias check and review date:** What contrary evidence is missing, and when will the entry be revisited?

At review, preserve the original entry and append observed facts, differences, error classification, process assessment, and one improvement for the next decision.

## Knowledge Check

1. **Multiple choice:** Which statement best describes a falsifiable thesis?  
   A. A company has a strong brand.  
   B. The price will rise because the company is popular.  
   C. A dated causal claim with observable supporting and invalidating conditions.  
   D. A summary of every favorable fact.

2. **True or false:** A variant perception is useful simply because it differs from the prevailing view.

3. **Short reasoning:** Why should evidence, interpretation, and assumptions be recorded separately?

4. **Calculation:** A hypothetical adverse scenario has a 25% probability and value of $30, a base scenario has a 50% probability and value of $60, and a favorable scenario has a 25% probability and value of $90. What is the probability-weighted value?

5. **Multiple choice:** Which item is normally the strongest primary source for a factual claim about a U.S. public company's audited annual financial statements?  
   A. An anonymous social-media post.  
   B. The company's Form 10-K filed on EDGAR.  
   C. A price chart without labels.  
   D. A repeated rumor.

6. **True or false:** A price decline automatically proves that the operating thesis is broken.

7. **Short reasoning:** What is the difference between a review trigger and a thesis-breaker?

8. **Multiple choice:** A hypothetical decision earns a profit after unrelated takeover news, but the learner ignored sources and exposure rules. What is the best process assessment?  
   A. The process was good because the outcome was profitable.  
   B. The process contained information and execution errors despite the favorable outcome.  
   C. No review is needed.  
   D. The thesis was necessarily correct.

9. **Short reasoning:** Why should the original decision-journal entry be preserved rather than rewritten after the outcome?

10. **True or false:** One correct 70%-confidence forecast is enough to prove that a learner is well calibrated.

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. **C.** A falsifiable thesis includes a date or horizon, a causal claim, observable evidence, and conditions that could weaken or invalidate it.
2. **False.** A different view must also be supported, material, and relevant to an expectation reflected in price.
3. **They have different levels of verifiability.** Evidence can be checked, interpretation explains meaning, and assumptions are uncertain inputs that require sensitivity testing and revision.
4. **$60 per share.** The calculation is \((0.25 \times \$30) + (0.50 \times \$60) + (0.25 \times \$90) = \$7.50 + \$30 + \$22.50 = \$60\). It remains an assumption-weighted estimate.
5. **B.** A Form 10-K on EDGAR contains audited annual financial statements and related disclosures, although the full filing and footnotes still require careful reading.
6. **False.** Price may change for many reasons. The learner should check evidence, risk, and predefined rules rather than treating price alone as proof.
7. **A review trigger requires fresh analysis; a thesis-breaker invalidates a central assumption or violates an essential risk condition.** Not every trigger leads to invalidation.
8. **B.** A favorable outcome does not erase weak research or a rule violation. Treating it as proof of good process would reflect outcome bias.
9. **Preservation protects the original information set.** It reduces hindsight reconstruction and allows the learner to compare what was expected with what actually occurred.
10. **False.** Calibration requires a sufficiently large set of comparable forecasts. One result is consistent with many possible levels of forecasting skill.

## Key Takeaways

- A useful investment thesis is concise, causal, dated, and falsifiable.
- Variant perception requires evidence and a clear comparison with an uncertain market expectation.
- Evidence, interpretation, forecasts, and assumptions should never be blended into one category.
- Scenarios and probability-weighted calculations organize uncertainty but do not eliminate it.
- Milestones, review triggers, and thesis-breakers serve different purposes.
- A decision journal preserves the information available before the outcome and makes hindsight reconstruction easier to detect.
- Profit and loss are important outcomes, but they do not by themselves measure decision quality.
- Process reviews should classify errors, recognize normal uncertainty, and identify one actionable improvement.
- Calibration is evaluated across repeated, consistently defined forecasts.
- Primary and authoritative sources should anchor factual claims, with dates and limitations recorded.

## Glossary

- **Investment thesis:** Testable explanation linking evidence, expected economics, valuation, and risk.
- **Market expectation:** Assumptions that appear consistent with price or prevailing forecasts.
- **Variant perception:** Supported interpretation differing materially from the relevant expectation.
- **Evidence:** Verifiable information relevant to a claim.
- **Interpretation:** Explanation of what evidence may mean.
- **Assumption:** Uncertain input accepted for analysis.
- **Scenario:** Coherent set of assumptions describing one possible path.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** Test of how conclusions change when inputs change.
- **Catalyst:** Event that may change expectations.
- **Milestone:** Observable checkpoint for thesis progress.
- **Review trigger:** Event requiring reassessment.
- **Thesis-breaker:** Evidence or rule violation invalidating the central case.
- **Decision journal:** Timestamped pre-outcome record of reasoning.
- **Hindsight bias:** Seeing an outcome as more predictable after learning it.
- **Outcome bias:** Judging process quality partly by the result.
- **Calibration:** Agreement between stated confidence and observed frequency across repeated forecasts.
