# Portfolio Construction and Diversification

> **Educational scope:** This document explains general portfolio concepts. It does not provide personalized investment advice, recommend a security, prescribe an allocation, or guarantee a return. Hypothetical examples use U.S. dollars only for clarity.

## Learning Objectives

- Translate a financial goal into a portfolio objective, horizon, liquidity need, and risk constraint.
- Distinguish asset allocation from diversification within an asset class.
- Explain how correlation and concentration affect portfolio-level risk.
- Describe position sizing as a method for limiting the effect of any one exposure.
- Identify geographic, sector, currency, employer, and fund-overlap exposures.
- Explain rebalancing methods, costs, and limitations.
- Recognize both under-diversification and over-diversification.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand stocks, bonds, cash, ETFs, volatility, liquidity, and the possibility of loss. Familiarity with percentages and weighted averages is helpful.

## Core Concepts

### Portfolio objective

A portfolio objective states what the money is intended to accomplish, when it may be needed, and which failures matter most. “Earn as much as possible” is incomplete because it omits the date, liquidity need, acceptable loss, and consequences of falling short.

A useful educational objective includes the goal amount or purpose, time horizon, contribution pattern, required liquidity, and risk constraints. Several goals may require separate portfolios because tuition due next year and retirement decades away do not share the same acceptable risk.

### Time horizon, risk capacity, and risk tolerance

The time horizon is the period before the money is expected to be used. Risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb loss without breaking the plan. Risk tolerance is the willingness to experience uncertainty and temporary loss without abandoning the strategy.

These are related but not identical. A long horizon can support more uncertainty, yet unstable income, high debt, a near-term obligation, or a concentrated employer exposure can reduce capacity. A portfolio should respect the more restrictive constraint rather than assuming that age or horizon alone determines the correct risk level.

### Asset allocation

Asset allocation divides a portfolio among broad categories such as stocks, bonds, and cash. The categories tend to have different expected return patterns, volatility, liquidity, and sensitivity to economic conditions. Allocation is therefore a major source of portfolio behavior.

Allocation is not a promise. A mix that historically had moderate volatility can still experience loss, and the relationship among asset classes can change. The purpose of an allocation is to express a planned balance among growth, stability, income, and liquidity.

### Diversification

Diversification spreads exposure across different securities, issuers, sectors, regions, currencies, maturities, credit qualities, and economic drivers. It can reduce the harm caused by a single company failure or a narrow shock.

Diversification cannot remove broad market risk, inflation risk, or every simultaneous decline. A diversified portfolio can lose money when many assets respond to the same recession, liquidity shock, or change in interest rates.

### Correlation

Correlation summarizes how two return series moved together over a historical sample. Values near positive one indicate that they usually moved in the same direction; values near negative one indicate opposite movement; values near zero indicate little linear co-movement in that sample.

Correlation is not permanent, causal, or sufficient by itself. It depends on the period, frequency, currency, and market regime. Assets that appeared weakly correlated in normal conditions may become more correlated during stress. Historical correlation is therefore evidence, not a guarantee.

### Concentration risk

Concentration risk arises when a large part of the portfolio depends on one security, issuer, sector, country, currency, factor, employer, or economic event. Concentration may be deliberate, may result from price appreciation, or may be hidden inside overlapping funds.

Counting ticker symbols does not measure diversification. An investor who owns a technology stock, two technology-heavy ETFs, and earns a salary from the same industry may have one combined exposure expressed through several accounts.

### Position sizing

Position sizing is the decision about how much of the portfolio is assigned to each holding or risk driver. It links an investment idea to the amount of damage that idea could cause if it performs badly.

There is no universal position-size percentage. Appropriate limits depend on liquidity, volatility, uncertainty, correlation with other holdings, tax consequences, account rules, and the importance of the goal. Educational analysis should identify both direct size and look-through exposure through funds.

### Rebalancing

Rebalancing brings a portfolio back toward its target allocation after market movement, contributions, or withdrawals create drift. Common approaches include calendar reviews, tolerance bands, directing new cash to underweight categories, or selling overweight categories.

Rebalancing is a risk-control process, not a forecast that recent winners must fall. It can create taxes, transaction costs, bid-ask costs, and opportunity costs. A written rule should specify targets, permitted ranges, review frequency, and exceptions before markets become emotional.

### Portfolio constraints

Constraints are limits that shape what the portfolio can reasonably hold. They can include liquidity needs, taxes, legal or account restrictions, minimum trade sizes, ethical preferences, currency obligations, debt, emergency reserves, expected withdrawals, and inability to sell employer shares.

A mathematically attractive allocation may be unusable if it ignores these constraints. Portfolio construction is therefore an exercise in meeting a real objective under limits, not merely maximizing an estimated return.

## Detailed Explanation

### Start with the objective, not the product

Portfolio construction begins with a goal rather than a list of securities. Define the purpose, date, required cash flow, contribution schedule, and consequence of a shortfall. Then separate assets needed soon from assets intended for long-term growth. This prevents a near-term bill from being funded by an asset that may be difficult to sell or may be temporarily depressed.

The objective should also define the measurement unit. A future obligation in dollars is different from one in euros because currency movement can change purchasing power even if the investment rises in its local market. The relevant risk is the mismatch between assets and liabilities, not only the volatility shown on a chart.

### Build the strategic allocation

A strategic allocation assigns target percentages to broad asset categories. Stocks may support long-term growth but can experience large declines. Bonds may provide income or lower volatility, yet they carry interest-rate and credit risks. Cash can provide liquidity but may lose purchasing power after inflation.

The educational task is not to label an asset class “safe” or “risky” in isolation. It is to determine the role each category serves and how the combination behaves relative to the goal. The same holding can be suitable for one objective and unsuitable for another because the horizons and cash-flow needs differ.

### Diversify across and within categories

Diversifying across asset classes is not enough when each class is internally concentrated. A stock allocation invested in one company remains exposed to that company. A bond allocation concentrated in one issuer remains exposed to its credit quality. Diversification should be examined both across categories and within each category.

Look-through analysis is essential for funds. Two broad-sounding ETFs may own many of the same large companies. A target-date fund may already contain several underlying funds, so adding similar funds can duplicate rather than diversify exposure. Prospectuses, fund holdings, sector weights, country weights, and maturity data help reveal overlap.

### Map geographic, sector, and currency exposure

A company’s listing country is not always the same as its economic exposure. Revenue, suppliers, costs, and financing may come from several regions. Similarly, a “global” fund can still be dominated by one country or sector.

Currency exposure matters when assets and future spending use different currencies. A foreign investment can rise in its local currency while producing a lower return after conversion. Conversely, currency movement can increase a home-currency return. Currency diversification may reduce dependence on one currency, but it also adds another source of uncertainty.

Sector diversification should focus on economic drivers rather than labels alone. Banks, insurers, and real-estate companies may all respond to interest rates in different ways. Technology firms may share sensitivity to capital spending, regulation, or valuation changes even when their products differ.

### Understand correlation without overtrusting it

Portfolio volatility depends not only on the volatility of each asset but also on how the assets move together. Lower historical correlation can create a smoother combined path because weakness in one holding may be offset by strength or stability elsewhere.

However, correlation estimates can be unstable. A short sample may omit recessions; a long sample may mix outdated market structures; monthly data can show a different relationship from daily data. Stress analysis should therefore ask what common shock could cause supposedly separate holdings to decline together.

### Control concentration through size and exposure limits

Position sizing can prevent one error from dominating the entire result. Suppose a $100,000 hypothetical portfolio assigns $25,000 to one company. A 60% decline in that company reduces the portfolio by $15,000 before considering other holdings. If the position were $5,000, the same decline would reduce the portfolio by $3,000. The example does not identify a correct percentage; it demonstrates that size converts security-level loss into portfolio-level loss.

Exposure limits can be expressed by issuer, sector, country, currency, credit quality, or liquidity. The relevant measure may include indirect holdings. A 4% direct position may actually represent a larger exposure when the same company is also held by multiple funds.

### Rebalance with a documented rule

Market movement changes portfolio weights. If a hypothetical 60% stock and 40% bond portfolio grows to 72% and 28%, its risk has changed even though no trade occurred. Rebalancing can sell part of the overweight category, buy the underweight category, or direct new contributions toward the underweight category.

Calendar rules are easy to administer but may trigger trades when drift is small. Band rules respond to material drift but require monitoring. Cash-flow rebalancing may reduce taxes and transaction costs, but it may be too slow for a large imbalance. The policy should also explain when not to rebalance, such as when a small trade would be uneconomic.

### Recognize over-diversification

More holdings do not always improve the portfolio. Over-diversification can occur when additional positions are too small to affect results, duplicate existing exposures, increase fees and administrative complexity, or make monitoring impossible. A portfolio may become an expensive imitation of a broad index without intentionally choosing that objective.

The problem is not a specific number of holdings. The question is whether each holding adds a distinct role, improves exposure balance, or satisfies a constraint. A holding that adds no meaningful diversification and no clear purpose may add complexity without improving the plan.

## Worked Examples

All examples are hypothetical and are not recommendations.

### Example 1: Separating two objectives

A learner has $12,000 for a tuition payment due in 18 months and $30,000 intended for retirement decades later. Combining the money into one aggressive portfolio could expose the tuition payment to a forced sale after a decline. Treating the objectives separately allows the near-term money to prioritize liquidity while the long-term money follows a different risk policy.

### Example 2: Hidden fund overlap

A hypothetical portfolio holds three ETFs with 40, 80, and 100 securities. The fund names suggest variety, but look-through data show that the same five companies represent 28%, 24%, and 21% of the funds. The portfolio has more than 200 reported positions, yet a significant portion depends on the same issuers.

### Example 3: Weighted currency exposure

A $50,000 portfolio has $20,000 in assets primarily exposed to U.S. dollars, $15,000 primarily exposed to euros, and $15,000 primarily exposed to other currencies. The approximate direct currency weights are 40%, 30%, and 30%. This map does not measure every corporate currency exposure, but it reveals that the portfolio result may differ from the return measured in the investor’s spending currency.

### Example 4: Rebalancing with new cash

A target of 60% stocks and 40% bonds becomes 66% and 34% in a $110,000 portfolio. The current amounts are $72,600 and $37,400. A new $10,000 contribution could be directed mostly or entirely to bonds, reducing the imbalance without selling. Whether this is sufficient depends on the policy bands and costs.

## Common Mistakes

- Selecting products before defining the objective and constraints.
- Treating risk tolerance as the same as financial risk capacity.
- Counting fund names instead of examining underlying holdings.
- Ignoring employer stock and salary as related exposures.
- Assuming foreign listing automatically creates geographic diversification.
- Using historical correlation as a permanent forecast.
- Letting a winning position grow without reviewing concentration.
- Rebalancing too frequently without considering taxes and costs.
- Holding many tiny positions that add complexity but little diversification.

## Common Misconceptions

### Diversification prevents loss

Diversification can reduce security-specific or narrow risks, but it cannot guarantee profit or prevent loss during broad market declines.

### A long time horizon permits unlimited risk

Horizon is only one input. Liquidity needs, income stability, debt, behavior, and the consequences of shortfall also affect risk capacity.

### Several ETFs are automatically diversified

Funds can overlap heavily, track similar indexes, or share the same sector, country, currency, or factor exposure.

### Rebalancing is market timing

A predetermined rebalancing rule restores target risk. Market timing changes exposure based on a forecast of near-term price direction.

### More holdings are always better

Additional holdings may duplicate exposures, increase fees, and make the portfolio harder to understand without materially reducing risk.

## Practical Application

Use this educational portfolio-design worksheet:

1. State each goal, target date, expected contributions, withdrawals, and spending currency.
2. Record liquidity needs and consequences of a shortfall.
3. Separate risk capacity from emotional risk tolerance.
4. Assign a role to each broad asset category.
5. Map issuer, sector, region, currency, maturity, credit, and employer exposures.
6. Measure direct and fund look-through concentration.
7. Write position-size or exposure limits without assuming they eliminate loss.
8. Choose a calendar, band, or cash-flow rebalancing rule.
9. Estimate taxes, fees, spreads, and administrative complexity.
10. Review the plan after a change in goals or constraints, not merely after recent market performance.

## Key Takeaways

- Begin with objectives, horizons, liquidity needs, and constraints rather than products.
- Asset allocation and diversification solve different parts of the portfolio problem.
- Count economic exposures, not only securities or fund names.
- Correlation is sample-dependent evidence and can change during stress.
- Position sizing limits the influence of one exposure but cannot eliminate loss.
- Rebalancing restores a planned risk structure and may create taxes or costs.
- Diversification has limits, and excessive complexity can become over-diversification.
- Review the process separately from the eventual market outcome.

## Glossary

- **Portfolio objective:** The purpose, horizon, cash-flow needs, and constraints of invested money.
- **Asset allocation:** Division of a portfolio among broad asset categories.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across different investments and risk drivers.
- **Correlation:** A statistic describing historical co-movement between two series.
- **Concentration risk:** Amplified exposure to a common issuer, sector, region, currency, or driver.
- **Position sizing:** Determining the weight assigned to a holding or exposure.
- **Rebalancing:** Returning portfolio weights toward planned targets.
- **Risk capacity:** Financial ability to absorb loss without breaking the plan.
- **Risk tolerance:** Willingness to experience uncertainty and loss.
- **Look-through analysis:** Examining the underlying holdings and exposures inside funds.
- **Constraint:** A legal, financial, liquidity, tax, ethical, or operational limit.
- **Over-diversification:** Complexity or duplication that adds little meaningful risk reduction.
