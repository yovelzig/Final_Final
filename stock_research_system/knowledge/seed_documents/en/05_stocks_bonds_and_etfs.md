# Stocks, Bonds, and ETFs

> **Educational scope:** This document explains general financial concepts. It does not provide personalized investment advice, recommend a security, or guarantee a return. Security rights, tax treatment, disclosure rules, and fund structures vary by jurisdiction and product.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain what ownership through common stock represents.
- Identify dividends and capital appreciation as possible, not guaranteed, return sources.
- Explain a bond through principal, coupon, maturity, credit risk, and interest-rate risk.
- Describe ETFs and compare index-tracking with active management.
- Evaluate expense ratios, liquidity, tracking difference, and diversification limitations.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The learner should understand assets, return, risk, and time horizon.

## Core Concepts

### Stock

A stock represents an ownership interest in a company. Shareholders may benefit from appreciation or dividends and bear business and market risk.

**Question to ask:** What rights, economic exposure, and concentration does the share create?

**Limitation:** Ownership does not guarantee dividends, voting influence, or recovery of purchase price.

### Common stock and ownership

Common stock usually represents a residual ownership claim and often carries voting rights. Creditors and other senior claims generally have priority if a company is liquidated. Shareholders own shares in the corporation, not direct title to specific corporate assets.

**Question to ask:** What voting, dividend, and liquidation rights apply to this share class?

**Limitation:** Rights can differ across companies, jurisdictions, and multiple share classes.

### Dividend

A dividend is a distribution a company may declare for shareholders. It can contribute to total return, but it may be reduced, suspended, or omitted.

**Question to ask:** Is the distribution supported by durable cash generation?

**Limitation:** A dividend is not a contractual payment like bond interest.

### Capital appreciation

Capital appreciation is an increase in market price. It becomes a realized gain only when the position is sold above its cost, before fees and taxes.

**Question to ask:** Did the price rise because business value improved, expectations changed, or valuation expanded?

**Limitation:** An unrealized gain can reverse, and a good company can be a poor investment at an excessive price.

### Bond

A bond is a debt security under which an issuer promises specified payments subject to its terms and ability to pay. Bondholders are lenders rather than owners and face credit, rate, liquidity, and inflation risks.

**Question to ask:** Who issued it, when does it mature, and what can interrupt payment?

**Limitation:** A bond can lose market value or default; fixed income does not mean fixed price.

### Principal or face value

Principal, also called face or par value, is the amount used for scheduled repayment and often for coupon calculation. The market purchase price can be above or below principal.

**Question to ask:** What amount is scheduled for repayment, and what price is paid today?

**Limitation:** Receiving face value does not guarantee a profit if the bond was bought at a premium or lost purchasing power.

### Coupon

A coupon is the stated interest payment on many bonds, usually expressed relative to face value. Coupon cash flow is one component of bond return.

**Question to ask:** Is the coupon fixed, floating, deferred, or absent?

**Limitation:** A high coupon can accompany high credit risk or a premium purchase price.

### Maturity

Maturity is the date principal is scheduled to be repaid. Time to maturity affects rate sensitivity, reinvestment needs, and planning.

**Question to ask:** Will the security be held to maturity, and can the issuer repay?

**Limitation:** Selling before maturity exposes the holder to market price.

### ETF

An exchange-traded fund pools assets and issues shares traded on an exchange. ETFs can provide convenient exposure to many securities, strategies, or sectors.

**Question to ask:** What does the fund actually hold, how concentrated is it, and what are its costs?

**Limitation:** An ETF can be narrow, leveraged, complex, illiquid, or trade away from NAV.

### Index-tracking and actively managed ETFs

An index-tracking ETF seeks to follow a specified index using disclosed rules and implementation methods. An actively managed ETF gives a manager discretion within the fund mandate. Either type can underperform, change exposures, or be concentrated.

**Question to ask:** What benchmark or strategy is used, and how is exposure obtained?

**Limitation:** Passive does not mean neutral, and active does not guarantee better results.

### Expense ratio

The expense ratio expresses recurring fund operating expenses relative to assets. It is generally reflected in performance rather than billed as a separate invoice.

**Question to ask:** What recurring costs are included, and what costs remain outside the ratio?

**Limitation:** Spreads, commissions, taxes, and portfolio transaction costs may also matter.

### Liquidity

Liquidity is the ability to trade without excessive delay or price impact. ETF liquidity depends on both ETF-share trading and the liquidity of underlying holdings.

**Question to ask:** How wide is the spread and how easily can underlying assets be traded?

**Limitation:** Historical volume does not guarantee liquidity during market stress.

### Tracking difference

Tracking difference is the fund return minus the benchmark return over the same period and on a consistent basis. Expenses, taxes, sampling, cash, and trading can contribute.

**Question to ask:** Are dates, currency, dividend treatment, and fee basis aligned?

**Limitation:** One period does not guarantee future tracking quality.

### Diversification limitations

Diversification depends on economic exposures, not only the number of holdings. Many securities can share the same sector, country, currency, factor, maturity, or credit risk.

**Question to ask:** Which common shock could affect many holdings together?

**Limitation:** Diversification cannot eliminate broad market or product-structure risk.

### Net asset value

NAV is fund assets minus liabilities divided by shares outstanding. It estimates per-share value of the underlying portfolio.

**Question to ask:** How does trading price compare with NAV and how liquid are holdings?

**Limitation:** Intraday market price may be above or below NAV.

### Credit risk

Credit risk is the possibility that a borrower fails to make promised payments. It affects expected bond cash flows and prices.

**Question to ask:** What is the issuer's capacity and priority of payment?

**Limitation:** Credit ratings are opinions, not guarantees.

### Interest-rate risk

Interest-rate risk is the sensitivity of a bond's price to changes in market yields. Existing fixed payments become more or less attractive as rates change.

**Question to ask:** What are duration, maturity, and coupon characteristics?

**Limitation:** Holding to maturity does not remove default or inflation risk.

## Detailed Explanation

### Return sources and legal claims

Stock total return can include price change and dividends. Bond return can include coupon payments, price change, reinvestment, and principal repayment. ETF return reflects underlying holdings, expenses, implementation, distributions, and the market price at purchase and sale.

The legal claim should be identified before comparing returns. Common shareholders hold a residual ownership claim; bondholders hold a contractual lending claim and generally rank ahead of common shareholders. Dividend yield and coupon rate are therefore not equivalent measures.

### Bond price and interest rates

Existing fixed-rate bond prices generally move inversely to comparable market yields. If new bonds offer higher yields, an older lower-coupon bond may need to trade at a lower price. Longer maturities and lower coupons often increase rate sensitivity, all else equal.

Holding an individual bond to maturity reduces concern about interim price changes only if the investor does not need to sell and the issuer pays as promised. Credit, inflation, call, reinvestment, and opportunity-cost risks remain. A typical bond ETF holds a changing portfolio and normally has no single date when an ETF shareholder receives a fixed principal amount.

### ETF structure, trading, and costs

ETF shares trade throughout the day at market prices. NAV is fund assets minus liabilities divided by shares outstanding. Market price can be above NAV at a premium or below NAV at a discount. Creation and redemption activity often helps align price and portfolio value, but alignment is not guaranteed.

A low expense ratio does not describe every cost. The bid-ask spread affects trading, while premiums, discounts, commissions, taxes, and implementation affect results. Index-tracking ETFs follow index rules; actively managed ETFs use manager discretion under a mandate. Both require review of holdings, concentration, turnover, and risk disclosures.

### Tracking and diversification

Tracking difference measures the return gap between a fund and its benchmark using the same period and return basis. The expense ratio can explain part of the gap, while sampling, taxes, cash balances, trading, and timing can explain the rest.

Counting holdings is not enough to establish diversification. A fund with many technology companies can remain exposed to the same industry shock. A bond ETF can hold many issuers but concentrate in long maturities or lower credit quality. Review top holdings and shared sector, country, currency, factor, maturity, and credit exposures.

## Integrated Concept Review

### Ownership versus lending

Stocks and bonds issued by the same company represent different claims. Common shareholders participate in residual gains and losses. Bondholders receive contractual payments if the issuer performs and usually have priority over common stock. The comparison should start with rights, cash flows, and priority before examining expected return.

### Maturity, horizon, and liquidity

An individual bond can schedule repayment, but selling early can produce a gain or loss. Stocks and ordinary ETFs have no promise to reach a target value by a chosen date. Liquidity means an asset can be traded; it does not guarantee a favorable price when money is needed.

### ETF wrapper versus underlying risk

The ETF wrapper provides pooled exposure and exchange trading, but it does not determine safety. An index can be concentrated, an active strategy can drift from its benchmark, and a bond ETF can fall when yields rise. Review the actual holdings or reference assets rather than relying on the product name.

### Costs and holding period

Recurring expenses matter over time, while spreads and premiums or discounts affect entry and exit. A lower expense ratio can be outweighed by worse trading conditions during a short holding period. Total friction should be evaluated for the intended trade size, horizon, and market conditions.

## Worked Examples

All amounts, entities, and outcomes below are hypothetical and are used only to demonstrate calculations or reasoning.

### Common-stock total return

An investor buys 10 shares at $40. One year later the price is $44 and $0.50 per share was paid in dividends.

- Price gain: `10 × ($44 - $40) = $40`
- Dividends: `10 × $0.50 = $5`
- Pre-fee total gain: `$45`
- Simple total return: `$45 ÷ $400 = 11.25%`

The ending price and dividend were not guaranteed.

### Bond coupon and purchase price

A bond has $1,000 face value and a 4% annual coupon, so it pays $40 annually under the assumed terms. If purchased for $950, the coupon rate remains 4% of face value, while simple current yield is about `$40 ÷ $950 = 4.21%`. This is not yield to maturity.

### Bond-rate effect

After comparable market yields rise, an older fixed-rate bond may trade at a lower price so its cash flows become more competitive. The exact change depends on maturity, coupon, credit, and liquidity.

### ETF tracking difference

An index returns 8.00% and an ETF tracking it returns 7.72% on the same total-return basis.

`Tracking difference = 7.72% - 8.00% = -0.28 percentage points`

The gap may reflect expenses and other implementation effects.

### Diversification limitation

A fund owns 100 stocks, but 65% of assets are in one industry and the ten largest positions represent 55%. The holding count is large, yet industry and top-position concentration remain.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating common stock as a promise to repay principal.
- Assuming dividends are guaranteed or equivalent to bond interest.
- Comparing coupon rate directly with stock total return.
- Assuming a bond bought above face value earns the coupon rate as investor return.
- Treating bonds or bond ETFs as risk free.
- Choosing an ETF by name without reading its objective and holdings.
- Treating index tracking as exact or costless.
- Comparing expense ratios while ignoring spreads and premiums or discounts.
- Counting holdings without examining common exposures.

## Common Misconceptions

### Common shareholders own specific company assets

They own shares in the corporation; the corporation owns its assets and owes its liabilities.

### Fixed income means fixed market value

The payment terms can be fixed while the bond or bond-fund market price changes.

### Holding to maturity removes every bond risk

It can reduce concern about interim price changes if payments occur and no sale is needed, but credit, inflation, call, and reinvestment risks remain.

### A bond ETF matures like one individual bond

Most bond ETFs hold changing portfolios and have no single principal-repayment date for the shareholder.

### Every ETF is diversified

Some track narrow sectors, themes, one security, leverage, or concentrated indexes.

### The lowest expense ratio always means the lowest cost

Trading spreads, premiums or discounts, taxes, and holding period also affect results.

## Practical Application

Use this educational checklist without treating the result as personal advice:

- Classify the product as ownership, lending, or pooled exposure.
- For common stock, record share-class rights, dividends, valuation assumptions, and concentration.
- For a bond, record issuer, face value, purchase price, coupon, maturity, seniority, call terms, and credit risk.
- For an ETF, inspect the objective, index or active strategy, actual holdings, expenses, spread, and premium or discount information.
- Compare fund and benchmark returns over identical dates to estimate tracking difference.
- Examine top holdings and common sector, country, currency, factor, maturity, and credit exposures.
- Match liquidity and price uncertainty to the goal horizon.
- Write the main loss scenario before considering a purchase.

## Key Takeaways

- Common stock represents ownership and a residual claim, not a repayment promise.
- Dividends and appreciation are possible return sources, not guarantees.
- Bonds are lending claims defined by principal, coupon, maturity, and contract terms.
- Credit risk concerns payment; interest-rate risk concerns price sensitivity to yields.
- ETFs are wrappers around portfolios or strategies, not one uniform risk category.
- Index-tracking and active ETFs can both underperform or be concentrated.
- Expense ratio, liquidity, spread, premium or discount, and tracking difference should be reviewed together.
- Diversification depends on shared economic exposures, not only the number of holdings.

## Glossary

- **Stock:** Security representing ownership in a corporation.
- **Common stock:** Equity class generally associated with voting rights and a residual claim.
- **Dividend:** Distribution a company may declare for shareholders.
- **Capital appreciation:** Increase in market price.
- **Bond:** Debt security representing a lending claim.
- **Principal / face value / par value:** Amount used for repayment and often coupon calculation.
- **Coupon:** Stated bond interest payment.
- **Maturity:** Scheduled principal-repayment date.
- **Credit risk:** Risk promised payments are not made in full and on time.
- **Interest-rate risk:** Bond-price sensitivity to market yields.
- **ETF:** Exchange-traded pooled investment fund.
- **Index-tracking ETF:** ETF seeking to follow a specified index.
- **Actively managed ETF:** ETF using manager discretion under a mandate.
- **Expense ratio:** Recurring fund operating expenses relative to assets.
- **Liquidity:** Ability to trade without excessive delay or price impact.
- **NAV:** Fund assets minus liabilities divided by shares.
- **Premium / discount:** Market price above / below NAV.
- **Tracking difference:** Fund return minus benchmark return for a consistent period.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across investments or risk drivers.
